# Appendectomy Recovery — Patient Guide

An appendectomy is surgery to remove the appendix, usually because of appendicitis (an inflamed or infected appendix). It is one of the most common emergency operations. This guide explains what to expect during recovery, how to care for your wound, manage pain, eat, move, and — most importantly — the warning signs that mean you should contact your care team.

> This is general education grounded in public-health sources. Always follow the specific instructions your surgeon gave you. If you have a medical emergency, seek urgent care.

## Types of appendectomy

There are two surgical approaches, and your recovery timeline depends on which you had:

- **Laparoscopic (keyhole) appendectomy** — performed through 2–3 small incisions using a camera. Most people go home within 24 hours and recover faster. Typical full recovery is about **1 to 3 weeks**.
- **Open appendectomy** — a single larger incision in the lower right abdomen, used when the appendix has burst or there is widespread infection (peritonitis). Recovery is longer, typically **2 to 4 weeks**, sometimes more if infection was present.

If the appendix had already burst, you may stay in hospital longer and receive intravenous antibiotics, because infection has spread into the abdomen.

## Recovery timeline (day by day)

### Day 0–1 (surgery and first 24 hours)
- You will feel drowsy from anaesthesia. Some shoulder-tip or abdominal pain from the gas used during laparoscopy is normal and settles in a few days.
- Start sipping fluids, then light food as tolerated. Get up and walk a little as soon as staff say it is safe — early walking prevents blood clots and helps the gut wake up.
- Mild nausea is common in the first day.

### Day 2–3
- Pain is controlled with simple painkillers. You can usually shower (keep the wound area gentle). Keep walking short distances around the home several times a day.
- Appetite begins to return. Bowel movements may not have happened yet — this is normal.

### Day 4–7
- Pain steadily decreases. Many people with keyhole surgery feel notably better by the end of the first week.
- Tiredness is expected — your body is healing. Nap when you need to. Continue gentle walking; avoid heavy lifting.

### Week 2
- Most laparoscopic patients return to light daily activities, office/desk work, and gentle routines. Open-surgery patients are usually a little behind this.
- Wound is healing; any dissolvable stitches are dissolving. Bruising around incisions fades.

### Week 3–6
- Gradual return to exercise, heavier work, and lifting as your surgeon advises. Most people are fully back to normal by 3–6 weeks. Do not rush — increasing pain is a signal to slow down.

## Wound and incision care

- **Keep incisions clean and dry.** You can usually shower 24–48 hours after surgery unless told otherwise. Pat the area dry; do not rub.
- **Avoid baths, swimming pools, and soaking** the wound until it is fully healed and your surgeon approves (often around 1–2 weeks).
- **Dressings:** follow the specific advice you were given. Small keyhole wounds may be covered with thin strips or glue.
- **Stitches:** many are dissolvable. If you have stitches or staples that need removal, you will be told when (often 7–10 days).
- It is normal to see a little redness right at the incision edges and feel numbness, tightness, or itching as it heals.
- Wash your hands before and after touching the wound area.

## Pain management and medication

- Mild to moderate pain is expected for several days and is well controlled with simple analgesia.
- **Paracetamol (acetaminophen)** is usually the first-line painkiller. Take it regularly at the recommended dose as directed.
- **Ibuprofen** (an anti-inflammatory) is often used as well to reduce pain and inflammation. Take it **with food** to protect your stomach. Avoid it if your surgeon told you not to (for example, certain kidney or stomach conditions).
- Stronger opioid painkillers are sometimes prescribed for the first few days only; they can cause constipation, so use them only as needed.
- If you were sent home with **antibiotics** (common after a burst appendix), finish the entire course even if you feel well.
- Never exceed the stated dose. If pain is getting worse rather than better, or is not controlled by your medication, contact your care team.

## Diet and nutrition

- Start with **fluids and light, easily digested foods** (toast, soup, rice, bananas) and build back to your normal diet over a few days as your appetite returns.
- **Stay well hydrated** — aim for regular water through the day. Hydration helps healing and prevents constipation.
- Eat **protein** (eggs, chicken, fish, beans, dairy) to support tissue repair, and **fibre** (fruit, vegetables, whole grains) to keep bowels moving.
- Painkillers (especially opioids) and reduced movement can cause **constipation**. Fibre, fluids, gentle walking, and a stool softener if advised will help. Straining is uncomfortable on a healing abdomen.
- There is usually no special long-term diet after an appendectomy — the appendix is not essential for digestion.

## Activity, movement, and restrictions

- **Walk a little, often** from day one — short, frequent walks prevent clots and speed recovery.
- **Avoid heavy lifting and strenuous activity** (lifting more than a few kilograms, intense exercise, heavy housework) for about **2 weeks after keyhole surgery** and **4–6 weeks after open surgery**, or as your surgeon advises.
- **Driving:** do not drive until you can perform an emergency stop without pain and are off strong painkillers — often around 1 week for keyhole, longer for open surgery, and check your insurer's rules.
- **Returning to work:** desk work is often possible within 1–2 weeks; physically demanding jobs need longer.
- **Sex and exercise:** resume gently when comfortable, usually after 1–2 weeks for light activity; ask your surgeon about contact sports and the gym.
- Support your abdomen with a hand or pillow when you cough, sneeze, or laugh in the first days.

## Warning signs — when to call your doctor

Most recoveries are smooth, but **contact your surgical team or seek urgent care** if you notice any of these. They can indicate infection, an abscess, or another complication:

- **Fever of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher**, chills, or feeling shivery and unwell.
- **Increasing or severe abdominal pain** rather than steadily improving pain.
- **Redness, swelling, warmth, or spreading red streaks** around an incision.
- **Pus, cloudy or foul-smelling discharge, or bleeding** from a wound, or a wound that opens up.
- **Persistent vomiting** or being unable to keep fluids down.
- **A hard, swollen, or bloated abdomen**, or **no bowel movement or passing of gas for more than 3 days** (possible bowel obstruction).
- **Diarrhoea lasting more than a few days.**
- **Pain, redness, or swelling in a calf**, or **shortness of breath / chest pain** (possible blood clot — seek emergency help).
- **Difficulty or pain when urinating**, or not passing urine.
- Loss of appetite that does not improve, or feeling generally worse after initially improving.

**Call emergency services** for severe chest pain, severe breathing difficulty, heavy bleeding, or collapse.

## When recovery is on track

Reassuring signs that you are healing normally: pain that decreases a little each day, returning appetite, passing wind and having bowel movements, incisions that look dry and are not spreading redness, and gradually increasing energy and activity tolerance.

## Frequently asked questions

**When can I shower?** Usually 24–48 hours after surgery; keep the wound gentle and pat dry. Avoid baths and swimming until healed and approved.

**Is it normal to feel tired?** Yes. Fatigue for one to two weeks is expected as your body heals. Rest and gentle activity both help.

**When will my bowels return to normal?** Often within a few days. Constipation from painkillers is common — fibre, fluids, and walking help.

**Do I need a special diet long term?** No. The appendix is not needed for digestion; you can return to your usual balanced diet.

**When can I exercise again?** Light activity in 1–2 weeks; heavier exercise and lifting usually after 2–6 weeks depending on the operation and your surgeon's advice.

**The pain is getting worse on day 5 — is that normal?** No. Pain should trend downward. Worsening pain, especially with fever, needs prompt medical review.
